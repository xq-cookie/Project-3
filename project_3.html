Are Songs Getting Shorter? My Deep Dive into Spotify Data (2009-2025)¶

Course: Computing in Context @ Columbia University
Author: Xi Qu
Date: December 2025

Why I Chose This Topic¶

As a music enthusiast who spends way too much time scrolling through TikTok and Spotify playlists, I've noticed something interesting: songs seem to be getting shorter. Those viral 15-second hooks on TikTok, the quick skips on Spotify, the pressure to grab attention immediately — it all got me thinking about how the streaming era might be reshaping the music we listen to.

I wanted to explore this phenomenon with real data. Specifically, I set out to answer:

Is there an "optimal" song duration that maximizes popularity, and has this relationship evolved from 2009 to 2025?

My Hypothesis¶

Based on what I've observed with short-form content dominating social media, I suspected that:

Songs between 3-4 minutes are the "sweet spot" for popularity, and this trend has become more pronounced in the streaming era.

Let's see if the data supports my hunch.

About the Data¶

I'm using the Spotify Global Music Dataset (2009-2025) from Kaggle, which contains:

  • track_data_final.csv: Classic hits from 2009-2023 (8,778 tracks)
  • spotify_data clean.csv: Modern tracks from 2025 (8,582 tracks)

The key columns I'll be working with are track_duration, track_popularity, album_release_date, and explicit.

Cleaning Up the Data¶

Looking at both datasets, I noticed a few inconsistencies I needed to fix:

  1. Duration format mismatch: The classic dataset has duration in milliseconds (track_duration_ms), while the modern one uses minutes (track_duration_min). I'll need to standardize this.

  2. Year extraction: I need to pull out the release year from album_release_date to analyze trends over time.

  3. Combining datasets: To get the full picture from 2009 to 2025, I'll merge both datasets together.

Let me fix these issues:

An Interesting Paradox: Significant But Weak¶

The results surprised me at first. Here's what I found:

  • Pearson Correlation: r = 0.103 (a weak positive correlation)
  • P-value: 2.5 × 10⁻⁴² (extremely statistically significant)

Wait — how can it be both statistically significant and weak? This is where sample size comes into play.

Because I'm working with 17,318 tracks, I have enough statistical power to detect even tiny effects. The correlation is statistically significant, meaning it's real and not just random noise. But it's practically weak — duration only explains about 1% of the variance in popularity (r² ≈ 0.01).

What this means for artists and music fans: Duration matters a little, but it's definitely not the main driver of a song's success. Other factors play much larger roles:

  • Artist popularity and existing fanbase
  • Marketing and playlist placement
  • Song quality and catchiness
  • Release timing and cultural moment
  • Genre trends

The scatter plot confirms this — while there's a slight upward trend, there's enormous variation at every duration point. A 2-minute song can be just as popular as a 5-minute song if other factors align.

I actually find this reassuring. It suggests that artists don't need to rigidly stick to a specific duration — they should focus on making a great song first.

Why Is This Happening?¶

After seeing these results, I started thinking about what's driving this change:

  1. Streaming economics: Spotify counts a "play" after 30 seconds, so there's less financial incentive for very long songs
  2. Attention spans: Competition for listener attention has intensified dramatically
  3. TikTok influence: Songs are often discovered through 15-60 second clips, favoring tracks with immediate hooks
  4. Skip culture: Easy skipping means songs need to hook listeners faster or risk being abandoned

One interesting observation from the line chart: While the overall trend favors shorter songs, the optimal duration within each era has remained relatively stable in the 3.5-4.5 minute range. This suggests there's a natural "floor" below which songs lose structural integrity — they still need verses, choruses, and bridges to feel complete.

In other words, songs are getting shorter, but there are limits to how short they can go while still being... well, songs.

What I Learned (And What Surprised Me)¶

After spending hours digging through 17,318 tracks spanning from 1952 to 2025, I walked away with a much better understanding of how song duration relates to popularity — and some of my assumptions were challenged along the way.

The Sweet Spot Exists, But It's Not Where I Thought¶

Going into this project, I was pretty confident that 3-4 minute songs would be the winners. That's the classic pop formula, right? Turns out I was close, but not quite right. The data showed that songs in the 3.5-4.5 minute range actually perform best, averaging 55-56 popularity points compared to just 35.6 for very short tracks under a minute. That's a 20-point advantage — significant in the streaming world where every point matters for playlist placement.

What surprised me most was realizing that duration matters... but not nearly as much as I expected. The correlation coefficient came out to just 0.103, which means duration only explains about 1% of why some songs become hits and others don't. At first, this felt like a letdown — I wanted to find a clear "magic number" that artists could aim for. But the more I thought about it, the more reassuring this became. It means that a great 2-minute song can absolutely compete with a 5-minute track. Song quality, artist reputation, marketing, and plain old luck matter far more than hitting some arbitrary length.

Songs Really Are Getting Shorter¶

This was the finding that felt most satisfying because it validated my original curiosity. Popular songs have shrunk by 42 seconds on average — from 4.04 minutes in the pre-streaming era to just 3.33 minutes in the TikTok age. That might not sound like much, but multiply it across millions of songs and you're looking at a fundamental shift in how music is structured.

I think the reasons are pretty clear when you consider how we consume music now. Spotify counts a play after just 30 seconds, so there's no financial incentive to make songs longer. TikTok discovery means songs need hooks that hit within 15 seconds. And let's be honest — we all skip tracks more easily than we used to. Artists and labels have adapted accordingly.

What's interesting, though, is that the optimal duration within each era stayed pretty stable. Even as songs got shorter overall, the sweet spot remained around 3.5-4.5 minutes. This tells me there's a natural floor to how short a song can be while still feeling complete. You still need verses, choruses, and some kind of arc. A song can't just be a hook on repeat — at least not yet.

Was I Right? Mostly, But Not Entirely¶

Looking back at my hypothesis — that 3-4 minute songs are the sweet spot and this trend has become more pronounced in streaming — I'd say I was partially right. The sweet spot exists, just slightly longer than I predicted. And the trend didn't become "more pronounced" so much as everything else shifted around it. Songs got shorter, but the ideal length stayed roughly the same.

I'll be the first to admit this analysis has limitations. The dataset might overrepresent certain genres or artists. Popularity scores are a snapshot in time and change constantly. And I couldn't control for confounding factors like marketing budgets or playlist placement. Most importantly, correlation isn't causation — I can't say that making your song 4 minutes long will make it more popular.

What This Project Taught Me¶

Beyond the specific findings, this project gave me a new appreciation for how technology shapes art in ways we don't always notice. That 42-second decline isn't just a number — it represents thousands of songwriters and producers making slightly different choices because of how streaming platforms work. It's a little bit sad, honestly, to think that economic incentives might be pushing music toward shorter, hookier formats.

But the data also gives me hope. Songs still need to be songs. There are limits to how short they can go while still resonating with people. And ultimately, duration explains so little of what makes a hit that artists shouldn't lose sleep over it. Make something great, and the length will sort itself out.

If I had more time, I'd love to dig into genre-specific patterns — does the optimal duration look different for hip-hop versus indie rock? I'm also curious whether songs with shorter intros get skipped less. But those are questions for another project.

Thanks for coming along on this journey with me. It was fun to finally put some data behind a question that's been rattling around in my head every time I notice a song fading out at the 2:45 mark.
